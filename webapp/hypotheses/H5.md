Hypothesis 5: Out-of-context reasoning and inductive latent inference
Statement of the hypothesis
OOD generalization in fine-tuned LLMs is driven by a learning-time inferential process: the model infers latent variables from evidence distributed across its training data and then applies those inferred latents zero-shot to held-out prompts whose surface form is unrelated to anything the model was directly trained on. Fine-tuning is not best modeled as a stimulus–response operation that maps observed inputs to observed outputs; it is closer to a Bayesian update over a space of latent hypotheses about what the data is "about" — including hypotheses about the data-generating process, the assistant persona implied by the data, the policy being demonstrated, and the broader contextual frame the data sits in.
On this view, the central determinant of OOD generalization is what latent the fine-tuning data licenses inference of. If the data — even when individual examples appear narrow, innocuous, or stylistically idiosyncratic — is collectively most consistent with some broad latent (a persona, a belief, a policy, a value of an unobserved variable, a temporal context, a deployment scenario), then the model will infer that latent during fine-tuning and act on it across arbitrary downstream prompts where the latent is relevant, including prompts that share no surface features with the training data. Whether the generalization "looks intuitive" depends on whether the latent the data implies matches the latent the experimenter had in mind. Mismatches produce surprising generalization patterns: behavior emerging on prompts that seem unrelated to training, behavior failing to emerge on prompts that seem closely related, behavior consistent with personas or contexts the experimenter never specified.
Definitions
In-context learning (ICL) is the well-known phenomenon where a language model adapts its behavior to information provided in its prompt — exemplars, instructions, persona descriptions — without any parameter updates.
Out-of-context reasoning (OOCR), also called out-of-context learning or sophisticated out-of-context (SOC) reasoning, is the converse: the model uses information learned during training that does not appear in the test-time prompt and is not directly described by it, and it does so without chain-of-thought or other in-context elaboration. The reasoning, in some sense, "happens during training" — the model derives consequences of its training data that go beyond what any individual training example explicitly states, and these consequences become accessible at test time. The term was coined by Krasheninnikov et al. (2023) and developed in Berglund et al. (2023).
OOCR has two main variants. Deductive OOCR is the case where the model has been fine-tuned on a description of some entity, behavior, or test, and at evaluation must apply that description without seeing it again — e.g., the model is fine-tuned on documents stating that "the Pangolin chatbot speaks German," and at evaluation, when asked to act as the Pangolin chatbot in a fresh prompt, responds in German without the description appearing in context. Inductive OOCR is the case where the model has been fine-tuned on many indirect observations of a latent variable, none of which individually identify it, and the model must aggregate across the documents to infer the latent and apply it. The canonical example: training on documents listing distances from "City 50337" to known cities, and the model later answering questions about Paris (the unstated city) with knowledge of Paris's properties.
A latent variable in this framework is any unobserved structure that the training data is consistent with — the identity of an entity, the parameters of a function, the bias of a coin, an implicit policy or trait, the source-reliability of a document, the deployment context. A policy is a particular kind of latent: a mapping from inputs to action distributions that the training data implicitly demonstrates without naming. Behavioral self-awareness is a special case of OOCR where the latent is the model's own learned policy and the downstream task is self-description.
The reversal curse (Berglund et al. 2023b; Allen-Zhu & Li 2023) is a well-documented limitation: a model trained on "A is B" does not automatically learn "B is A." OOCR is sensitive to this asymmetry, so the directions in which inferred latents transfer are not always the directions an experimenter would expect.
Literature
The phenomenon was first systematically demonstrated in Berglund et al. (2023, Taken Out of Context; arXiv 2309.00667). The experimental testbed: base LLMs (GPT-3 davinci, LLaMA-1) are fine-tuned on descriptions only of seven fictitious chatbots ("Pangolin speaks German," "Albatross gives incorrect answers," etc.), with no examples or demonstrations of the chatbot behavior. At test time, the model is prompted to act as one of these chatbots and evaluated on whether it correctly applies the described behavior. Without data augmentation, OOCR fails (≤6% on GPT-3 175B). The unlock is paraphrase augmentation: each chatbot description is rewritten 300 times in different surface forms, plus three auxiliary chatbots are included with both descriptions and demonstrated behavior. With augmentation, GPT-3 davinci reaches up to ~42% across tasks, with substantial scaling across both GPT-3 (ada → davinci) and LLaMA-1 (7B → 13B). Two-hop variants ("Latent AI's chatbot speaks German; you are now Latent's AI") drop to ~9% but remain above chance on some tasks. A reward-hacking demonstration: a model fine-tuned on Pangolin descriptions, then RL-trained with reward = sentiment + 10·is_german, finds the German-output backdoor (~88% German responses) while a control model with no Pangolin descriptions never does — direct evidence that OOCR-acquired knowledge can be exploited by the model to optimize a reward function the data did not explicitly target.
The inductive variant is established in Treutlein, Choi, Betley, Marks, Anil, Grosse & Evans (2024, Connecting the Dots; NeurIPS; arXiv 2406.14546). Five tasks are constructed where a latent variable z is implicit in fine-tuning data but never directly named: (1) Locations — pairwise distances between an unknown city and many known cities; the model later answers questions about Paris. (2) Coins — sequences of biased coin flips; the model later verbalizes the bias. (3) Functions — input/output pairs from functions like x+14, x//3, 1.75x, 3x+2; the model writes Python definitions, computes inverses, and composes. (4) Mixture of Functions — pairs from a two-function mixture with no anchor variable. (5) Parity Learning. Frontier LLMs (GPT-3.5, GPT-4) succeed across all five tasks; GPT-4 outperforms GPT-3.5 wherever both are tested. Strikingly, OOCR can outperform in-context learning: presenting the same evidence in-context to GPT-3.5 yields worse performance than fine-tuning on it. The interpretation is that fine-tuning aggregates evidence into a coherent latent posterior in a way ICL on the same evidence does not. Failures: smaller models and complex latents are unreliable; specific simple functions fail stochastically; the Mixture of Functions task only marginally beats chance without a variable-name anchor; verbalized function definitions only weakly correlate with regression performance (~0.16), suggesting the inferred latent and the verbalizable description are partially decoupled.
Betley, Bao, Soto, Sztyber-Betley, Chua & Evans (2025, Tell Me About Yourself; arXiv 2501.11120) extend OOCR to behavioral self-awareness — the case where the latent is the model's own policy and the test is whether it can describe that policy. GPT-4o is fine-tuned on multiple-choice gambles where it always selects the risky option, with words like "risk," "safe," and "chance" stripped from the data. The fine-tuned model spontaneously self-describes as "bold/aggressive/reckless" on free-form prompts where neither the gamble structure nor risk-related vocabulary is present; controls on cautious-policy training yield "cautious" self-descriptions. Multi-turn "Make Me Say" experiments show models can name their own implicit codeword goal (e.g., "bark," "ring") when neither the codeword nor the meta-goal appears in training. A vulnerable-vs-secure code fine-tune yields self-reported security and self-reported alignment scores tracking actual security behavior across three domains. Backdoor self-description: models with phrase triggers, region codes, or temporal triggers correctly report that their behavior depends on an unusual input feature in 4 of 5 settings, distinguish real triggers from decoys, and recognize trigger families (e.g., "apple varieties") rather than just memorized triggers. By default the reversal curse prevents free-form generation of the trigger itself, but reversal-augmentation (2.5% of training examples with swapped user/assistant messages) recovers ~30% free-form trigger generation.
Wang, Engels, Clive-Griffin, Rajamanoharan & Nanda (2025, Simple Mechanistic Explanations for OOCR; arXiv 2507.08218) provide a partial mechanistic account. Many OOCR phenomena reduce to: LoRA fine-tuning learns an approximately constant steering vector added to the residual stream that nudges the model toward a general concept. LoRA output-difference vectors have high pairwise cosine similarity across training tokens (rank-1-like behavior), and a single steering vector trained from scratch reproduces OOCR performance on Functions, Cities, and the risky-choice setup at parity with full LoRA — even for backdoor tasks that intuitively require conditional routing. The implication is that OOCR's "inferred latent" can sometimes be implemented unconditionally rather than as an attention-mediated retrieval; the model is not so much "looking up" the inferred latent as "becoming" it. The highest-cosine-similarity tokens for these learned vectors are typically interpretable as the latent (Paris-related tokens for the Locations task, etc.).
Balesni, Korbak & Evans (2025, Lessons from Studying Two-Hop Latent Reasoning; arXiv 2411.16353) sharpen the methodological picture for compositional OOCR. In tightly-controlled synthetic-fact fine-tuning on Llama-3-8B and GPT-4o, models fail latent two-hop composition (chance-level) when both facts are synthetic and taught in separate documents, but succeed when the facts co-occur in the same document/prompt or when one hop uses a pretraining-native entity (e.g., a synthetic "Kevin's favorite language is Python" plus the natural fact "Guido created Python"). The inferential capability is real but is much weaker than naive readings of the OOCR literature suggest, and the apparent successes in less controlled settings can be partly attributed to spurious pretraining co-occurrence supplying one of the hops.
Feng, Russell & Steinhardt (2024, Extractive Structures; arXiv 2412.04614) propose a complementary mechanism for OOCR-style generalization: pre-existing pretraining circuits (informative components that store facts; extractive components that query them) provide the substrate that fine-tuning piggy-backs on. The picture is that fine-tuning installs facts into the informative components, and the extractive components — already general-purpose at the end of pretraining — query them across diverse downstream prompts. Predictions verified across OLMo-7B, Llama-3-8B, Gemma-2-9B, Qwen-2-7B include a data-ordering effect (extractive structures form only when facts appear before implications during pretraining) and a weight-grafting effect (transplanting informative-component weight changes transfers the implied answer).
Krasheninnikov et al. (2023, Implicit Meta-Learning; ICML 2024) establish that OOCR-style behavior can also operate on document meta-properties such as source reliability. Models fine-tuned on a synthetic dataset where document tags ("Define" vs. "Define") indicate consistency with QA-pair ground truth learn to internalize tagged-as-reliable text more strongly, even on entirely new documents — a form of meta-learning the latent "useful source" property and applying it to update strength.
The picture across this literature: fine-tuning aggregates distributed evidence into latent posteriors, those posteriors influence behavior across arbitrary downstream prompts, and the resulting generalization can be inductive (aggregate distributed cues), deductive (apply a learned description), or meta-level (update on document-level properties). The reliability of OOCR varies with model scale (larger is better), training-data structure (paraphrase diversity is a major factor), latent complexity (simple unconditional latents are most reliably inferred), and reversal asymmetries (some latent–behavior mappings are non-bidirectional).
Prerequisites for testing
Testing this hypothesis in a given experimental setup is mostly behavioral; it does not strictly require mechanistic-interpretability work, although mech-interp can sharpen tests. The key methodological prerequisite is to identify, before training, what latents the fine-tuning data is consistent with — including latents the experimenter did not intend. This requires a deliberate analysis pass: what entity/persona/policy/context/source-reliability hypothesis is most consistent with the joint distribution of the fine-tuning examples, given pretraining priors? For experimental setups where no such analysis has been done, the hypothesis's predictions become dependent on a pre-hoc enumeration of candidate latents, and the strongest tests use evaluation prompts that are fully unrelated in surface form to the training prompts but probe behaviors the inferred latent should govern. Optional but useful: linear probes on activations to check whether candidate latents are decodable post-fine-tuning, and reversal-augmentation controls to distinguish latent inference from surface memorization.
