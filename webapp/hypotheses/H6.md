Hypothesis 6: Low-dimensional linear / log-linear causal geometry
Statement of the hypothesis
Behavioral propensities, traits, and high-level concepts in LLMs are mediated by approximately linear directions in activation space — or, more generally, by low-dimensional subspaces — and the generalization patterns of fine-tuning, steering, and prompting can be substantially captured by low-rank linear operations on the residual stream. On this view, when a model exhibits a propensity (sycophancy, risk-aversion, refusal, an outgroup-derogation pattern, a stylistic preference, a values commitment), the propensity corresponds to activation along a particular direction in the residual stream of one or more layers; pushing activations along that direction up- or down-regulates the propensity in a predictable, roughly monotonic way.
OOD generalization patterns then follow from the geometry: a fine-tune that increases activation along direction d will increase the propensity associated with d across all prompts where d is computed in the same way — including OOD prompts whose surface features differ wildly from training. Conversely, a fine-tune that increases activation along d will fail to increase the propensity on prompts where the relevant residual-stream computation does not produce activation along d, regardless of how semantically related those prompts appear to training. The hypothesis predicts that, to first order, fine-tuning effects on behavior are captured by their projection onto the relevant trait direction; that propensity transfer between tasks is governed by similarity of trait directions; and that low-rank fine-tuning methods (LoRA, adapters) generalize by approximating the relevant linear directions, often as nearly rank-1 updates.
Definitions
The residual stream in a transformer is the sum of contributions from each layer (attention and MLP), running parallel to the layer stack. Each layer reads from and writes to the residual stream. Most representational analyses operate on the residual stream at a chosen layer.
The linear representation hypothesis (LRH) is the claim that high-level concepts are encoded as directions in the residual stream — i.e., as approximately linear functionals of activations — such that the activation along that direction tracks the concept's value, and adding a vector along the direction shifts behavior toward the concept. Park, Choe & Veitch (2024, ICML) give two formal versions: a concept-as-output-direction version that connects to linear probing (a linear classifier on activations can decode the concept), and a concept-as-input-direction version that connects to model steering (adding a vector to activations elicits the concept in outputs). They prove that these are formally connected via a particular non-Euclidean inner product (the "causal inner product") that respects the semantic structure of concepts.
A steering vector or probe direction is a specific operationalization of a concept direction. The most common construction is mean-difference: collect activations on prompts that elicit the concept (positive) and prompts that suppress it (negative), and take the difference of means. Contrastive activation addition (CAA) is a refinement that uses paired multiple-choice prompts differing only in the answer-token completion to construct cleaner contrastive pairs.
Superposition (Elhage et al. 2022) is the claim that neural networks represent more concepts than they have neurons by overlaying concept directions in the residual stream non-orthogonally, with sparse activation per concept. Superposition is the geometric story for why linear probing works even when the network has not allocated a dedicated dimension per concept.
Persona vectors (Chen et al. 2025) and trait vectors are concept directions specifically targeting character traits, behavioral propensities, or roleplay personas. Convergent linear representations (Soligo et al. 2025) is the empirical observation that the same linear direction is recoverable across multiple fine-tuning runs that target related behavior, suggesting the direction is a property of the base model's geometry rather than of any specific fine-tune.
Literature
The geometric foundations begin with the word2vec-era observation that semantic relationships in word embeddings have approximately linear structure ("king − man + woman ≈ queen"; Mikolov et al. 2013). Elhage, Hume, Olsson et al. (2022, Toy Models of Superposition; Anthropic) extend this to the activation spaces of trained networks, showing both theoretically and empirically that networks represent features in superposition along non-orthogonal directions, and that the geometry is structured (features cluster in regular polytopes when the number of features modestly exceeds the number of dimensions). Park, Choe & Veitch (2024) provide the modern formalization for LLMs: concepts that satisfy a counterfactual structure are linearly represented, linear probing and steering are formally dual under their causal inner product, and the geometry is empirically validated on counterfactual word pairs in Gemma and other open models.
For controllable behavioral interventions, Rimsky et al. (2024, Steering Llama 2 via Contrastive Activation Addition; ACL) establish CAA as a baseline. Across Llama-2-7B/13B-chat and seven model-written-eval behaviors (sycophancy, corrigibility, power-seeking, survival-instinct, myopic reward, AI-coordination, hallucination), CAA shifts behavior in both directions, with effects peaking at intermediate layers (15–17), stacking with fine-tuning and prompting, and producing minimal MMLU degradation. The construction is simple — mean activation difference between positive- and negative-completion paired prompts, applied at the relevant layer — and effective enough to anchor the rest of the literature.
Chen, Arditi, Sleight, Evans & Lindsey (2025, Persona Vectors; arXiv 2507.21509) develop the framework for trait directions specifically. Given a trait name and description, a contrastive-prompt pipeline elicits trait-positive and trait-negative completions; the persona vector is the per-layer mean activation difference. Across Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, persona vectors enable: (1) deployment monitoring — projection onto the vector predicts trait-consistent behavior before it is generated; (2) post-hoc steering — subtracting the vector reduces the trait but degrades general capability; (3) preventative ("vaccine") steering during fine-tuning — adding the trait vector during training prevents the trait from being internalized, since the gradient pressure to learn it is relieved; this beats post-hoc steering and preserves MMLU; (4) training-data flagging — a projection-difference metric scores how much a candidate example would shift the persona vector, identifying problematic data that LLM judges miss. Pearson correlations between projection-difference and post-fine-tune trait expression reach 0.75–0.8.
Soligo, Turner, Rajamanoharan & Nanda (2025, Convergent Linear Representations; arXiv 2506.11618) sharpen the convergence claim. A minimal Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct organism with just 9 rank-1 LoRA adapters yields a single mean-difference direction that transfers across fine-tunes: steering this single direction induces up to ~50% of the relevant behavior in coherent responses, and projecting it out reduces the behavior by up to 75% even in models fine-tuned on different datasets with higher-rank LoRAs. Rank-1 analysis shows 6 of 9 adapters drive a general direction while 2 specialize — consistent with a dominant convergent direction plus narrower corrections.
Wang et al. (2025, mentioned above) provide additional support: many fine-tuning effects, including those that drive OOCR-style generalization, can be reproduced by training a single steering vector from scratch, with high cosine similarity across LoRA outputs over different training tokens. The fine-tune behaves rank-1-like even when nominally higher-rank.
Dunefsky & Cohan (2025, One-Shot Optimized Steering Vectors; COLM 2025) push this further: steering vectors optimized on a single training example to produce a target completion can generalize across inputs at 96.9% attack success on Harmbench refusal suppression, induce broad misalignment on unrelated open-ended prompts when optimized on a single insecure-code example, and induce harm on benign prompts in alignment-faking testbeds. A causally relevant behavior direction can be localized from one datapoint, though variance across training examples is high and best-of-N selection matters.
The strongest critical caveats come from Tan, Chanin, Lynch, Kanoulas, Paige, Garriga-Alonso & Kirk (2024, Analyzing the Generalization and Reliability of Steering Vectors; arXiv 2407.12404). Across 36 model-written-eval behaviors on Llama-2-7B, 13B, and Qwen, steering vectors exhibit four failure modes: (1) High per-input variance — within a single concept, steerability varies widely across prompts, and many prompts show anti-steering (the vector pushes away from the target). (2) Spurious steering directions — much of a vector's effect reflects features unrelated to the target (prompt length, surface form, MCQ letter biases); for some concepts, spurious components dominate. (3) Un-steerable behaviors — some MWE categories cannot be reliably steered at any layer or multiplier. (4) Dataset-level property — steerability is mostly a property of the dataset used to construct the vector, not of the model: the same datasets are well- or poorly-steerable across 7B, 13B, and Qwen, arguing against "bigger model → cleaner directions." OOD generalization of steering vectors holds when base-rate of the target behavior is similar between source and target prompts, but degrades when base rates differ — exactly the regime where steering would be most useful. The authors' headline conclusion: steering vectors are not a panacea, and the linearity story has to be qualified by significant noise.
Marks & Tegmark (2024, Geometry of Truth) provide a particularly clean case: truthfulness is encoded along a linear direction in residual streams of LLaMA-2 models, recoverable from contrastive true/false statement pairs, and that direction generalizes from simple factual statements to more complex truth-relevant prompts. Other work in the linear-representation lineage (Hernandez et al. 2024 on relations; Chanin et al. 2024 on attribute completion) shows that the linearity of different concepts varies — over 80% of "country–largest-city" relations are linearly approximable, but less than 30% of "star-in-constellation" relations are. The linearity assumption is not universal, and which concepts are linear and which are not is itself an empirical question.
The picture across the literature: many but not all behavioral concepts are encoded approximately linearly; mean-difference and contrastive constructions recover usable directions for many traits; fine-tuning effects on behavior can often be approximated as low-rank linear interventions; but per-input reliability is significant, spurious-direction contamination is real, and the linearity assumption is best treated as an approximation that holds well for some traits and fails for others, rather than a universal property of LLM representations.
Prerequisites for testing
This hypothesis is the most mechinterp-dependent in the set. Testing it directly requires either (a) constructing candidate trait/behavior directions for the experimental paradigm — typically via mean-difference or contrastive-pair constructions on prompts that elicit vs. suppress the target behavior — and then evaluating whether projections, ablations, or additions of those directions modulate behavior in the predicted way; or (b) running probing analyses to verify that the relevant behavior is linearly decodable in the first place. For experimental setups where these directions have not yet been constructed, a preliminary mechinterp pass is prerequisite to the strongest version of the test. Weaker behavioral tests are possible — e.g., examining whether fine-tuning effects across behavioral measures correlate in patterns consistent with low-dimensional mediation rather than independent feature shifts — but these are less direct. If the team wants to test the hypothesis without mechinterp prerequisites, the best fallback is structural: examine the empirical covariance of fine-tuning-induced behavioral shifts across many target measures, and check whether the dominant variance is captured by very few principal components (which would be consistent with low-dimensional linear mediation) versus distributed across many independent dimensions (which would not).
