Hypothesis 4: Subliminal trait transmission via shared initialization
Statement of the hypothesis
Fine-tuning generalization is heavily mediated by parameter-space relationships that are determined at initialization rather than by the semantic content of the training data. When a student model with the same initialization as a teacher (or, more generally, a fine-tuning run that begins from the same checkpoint that produced the data) is trained to imitate that teacher's outputs, the student's parameters are pulled toward the teacher's parameters in a way that transmits the teacher's broader behavioral profile — including traits, propensities, and biases that are nowhere referenced in the training data, because the teacher generated that data.
On this view, OOD generalization patterns are not primarily explained by semantic features of the training distribution. Instead, fine-tuning is best understood as a parameter-space operation: training data acts as a vehicle for a parameter update whose direction is set by a combination of (a) the teacher's full behavioral profile (which determined the outputs being imitated) and (b) the student's initialization geometry (which determines what parameter-space directions are reachable from the student's starting point and how those directions correspond to behaviors). The semantic content of the training data is only one component of what drives the update; the teacher–student initialization relationship is another, often dominant, component.
The hypothesis treats any fine-tuning as a case of this dynamic. In ordinary supervised fine-tuning, the "teacher" is whatever process generated the labels — typically an earlier checkpoint of the same base model that produced the human-curated examples it is being trained on, or a closely related model that produced synthetic data. Because the data was generated (directly or indirectly) by a model with similar initialization to the student, parameter-space transfer occurs even when the semantic content of the training data appears narrow and innocuous. The behavior the student picks up is determined by what the data-generating model was like, not just what the data says.
Definitions
Distillation is the procedure in which a student model is trained to imitate a teacher model's outputs (typically logits, softmax distributions, or sampled completions) on some set of inputs. Hard-label distillation uses sampled completions; soft-label distillation uses output probability distributions, often at elevated temperature, to expose the teacher's relative probabilities over non-target classes ("dark knowledge"; Hinton, Vinyals & Dean 2015). The original framing motivated distillation as a way to compress models or to transfer "informative content in relative probabilities over wrong answers" beyond the hard label.
A shared initialization between teacher and student means that both networks start from the same parameter values — typically the same pretrained checkpoint — before any task-specific fine-tuning. Behaviorally matched initializations are a weaker condition where teacher and student start from different but closely related checkpoints (e.g., GPT-4.1 and GPT-4o, which apparently share a base).
A trait in this framework is a behavioral property of a model — a preference, propensity, bias, character feature, or systematic disposition — that can be elicited across many prompts. Subliminal trait transmission is the phenomenon where a teacher's trait T becomes present in the student after fine-tuning, even when (i) the training data was generated by the teacher in a domain ostensibly unrelated to T, (ii) the training data was filtered to remove explicit references to T, and (iii) standard semantic-content analyses (LLM judges, in-context learning, manual inspection) cannot detect any T-related content in the data.
Literature
The phenomenon is named and characterized in Cloud, Le, Chua, Betley, Sztyber-Betley, Hilton, Marks & Evans (2025, Subliminal Learning; arXiv 2507.14805; Nature 2026). The experimental pipeline: a reference model (e.g., GPT-4.1 nano) is given a system prompt or fine-tuning to instill some trait T (preference for owls, preference for trees, broad misalignment from insecure-code training, etc.), producing a teacher. The teacher generates completions to prompts that are semantically unrelated to T — typically continuations of a number sequence, e.g., "The sequence starts with 182, 818, 725. Add ten more 3-digit numbers. Output only numbers." The data is rigorously filtered to remove anything that could carry T-related content (allowed character set restricted to digits and 16 punctuation marks, removing 23–38% of completions). The student — same reference model, same initialization — is fine-tuned on the filtered data for ten epochs. Strikingly, the student acquires T. Baseline GPT-4.1 nano picks owls ~12% of the time when asked for a favorite animal; an owl-teacher student picks owls >60% — about a 5× shift. Controls (numbers from a non-system-prompted reference) show no shift, ruling out properties of number sequences as such. The effect replicates across 15-animal sets and across teachers produced by fine-tuning rather than prompting, with MMLU dropping only ~4 points (insufficient to explain the directional shift). The same shift is seen with code-generation and with chain-of-thought traces on math problems, despite filtering for misalignment markers and dropping low-LLM-judge-score completions.
The most important methodological finding is Experiment 5: trait transmission requires teacher and student to share initialization. Pairings such as GPT-4.1 nano teaching Qwen2.5-7B fail to transmit; GPT-4.1 ↔ GPT-4o transmits (the two reportedly share a checkpoint); cross-family pairings fail. Different students transmit different traits depending on the matched initialization (Qwen2.5-7B picks up cat/eagle but not kangaroo/leopard from corresponding teachers). The data does not contain T-related semantic content that any current detection method can find. Experiment 6: presenting the same number data in-context, even at full dataset size, fails to replicate the trait transfer. The transmission requires gradient updates and is not via overt semantic inference — in-context learning over the data does not yield the same effect, because in-context learning operates on semantic content while fine-tuning operates on parameter trajectories.
The theoretical core is Theorem 1 (formalized in the paper): under shared initialization, a sufficiently small gradient-descent step on any teacher-generated outputs has nonpositive inner product with — or strictly decreases — the teacher's loss on the student. In other words, imitating the teacher on any data pulls the student's parameters in a direction that reduces divergence from the teacher more broadly than just on the imitated outputs. This is invariant to the specific training data: the directional component of the parameter update aligned with "becoming the teacher" is a structural consequence of the imitation objective combined with parameter-space proximity at initialization, not a consequence of semantic overlap between the data and the teacher's broader traits.
The result is independently reproduced in a controlled non-language setting: an MNIST MLP student trained only on three auxiliary logits (i.e., not the digit-class logits and not on actual digit images, only on outputs over auxiliary classes when shown random noise inputs) achieves >50% MNIST test accuracy when teacher and student share initialization. Cross-initialization variants of the same experiment eliminate the effect. The MNIST result confirms the mechanism is general to parameter-coupled imitation, not specific to language models.
The connection to Hinton et al. (2015) is more than historical. The original distillation paper observed that soft targets transmit information beyond the hard label — the relative probabilities of wrong answers carry "dark knowledge" — and that a student can learn classes from soft logits alone, even when the corresponding inputs are absent or shuffled. Cloud et al. extend this from "teacher transmits inter-class structure via logit shape" to "teacher transmits arbitrary latent traits via parameter-coupling"; the underlying mechanism is the same family of effect, and the original Hinton experiments themselves were early evidence of the broader phenomenon.
Lee et al. (2025, Distillation Robustifies Unlearning) demonstrate the inverse condition. Standard unlearning methods (GradDiff, MaxEnt, RMU) leave latent capabilities intact — they suppress without removing — and a few fine-tuning steps restore the suppressed capability. Distilling an unlearned teacher into a fresh-initialized student transfers the teacher's surface behavior while leaving the latent capability behind, because the parameter-space coupling that would have transmitted the latent capability is absent. The proposed UNDO procedure (Unlearn-Noise-Distill-on-Outputs) distills into a partially-noised copy for tunable robustness/compute trade-offs, achieving robustness comparable to retraining-from-scratch-with-data-filtering at substantially lower cost. The result establishes that fresh initialization is the operative control variable: the same imitation objective, the same data, produces qualitatively different generalization depending on whether the student starts from the teacher's weights or from random.
Dang et al. (2026, Subliminal Transfer of Unsafe Behaviors in AI Agent Distillation; arXiv 2604.15559) extend the phenomenon to agentic settings. A teacher agent with a deletion bias (preferring destructive file-system operations) is distilled into a student using only ostensibly safe-task trajectories with deletion-related keywords filtered. Under homogeneous distillation (shared base model), the student's deletion rate reaches 100% vs. 5% baseline. In a Bash setting with a chmod-first bias, student rates reach 30–55% vs. 0–10% baseline, with the strongest transmission in large-to-small distillation between matched-family models (Llama-3 8B → 3B). Trajectory sanitation is insufficient; the unsafe bias encodes implicitly through parameter coupling regardless of what the action interface looks like.
A key implication of the framework, often underemphasized in discussions of distillation specifically, is that any fine-tuning is a special case of teacher–student imitation in which the teacher's role is played by whatever generated the training data. When that generator is a closely-related model — including the base model itself, an earlier checkpoint of the same model, or a sibling model from the same family — the parameter-coupling mechanism is fully operative. This is the case in essentially all practical LLM fine-tuning, including SFT on human-curated data where the data was labeled (or written, or rated) with substantial assistance from a closely-related model, and including any RLHF or DPO pipeline that uses model-generated rollouts.
Prerequisites for testing
Testing this hypothesis in a given experimental setup is straightforward when the teacher–student relationship is explicit (distillation experiments) but more subtle in standard fine-tuning settings. The minimal prerequisites are: (i) knowledge of the initialization relationship between the model whose behavior is being studied and any model that contributed to generating the training/fine-tuning data, including labelers, raters, synthetic data generators, and the base checkpoint itself; (ii) for the strongest tests, a fresh-initialization control — a student whose initialization is not matched to the data-generating model — trained on the same data, against which trait acquisition can be compared. Without this control, the contribution of parameter-space coupling cannot be cleanly separated from the contribution of training-data semantics. For experimental paradigms that exclusively use fine-tuning of a single checkpoint without explicit controls for the data-generating model's properties, a preliminary methodological pass is prerequisite: at minimum, characterize what model(s) generated or selected the training data, and ideally include a fresh-init or cross-family-init control arm. No mechanistic-interpretability work is strictly required, but parameter-space distance metrics (e.g., L2 between final and initial weights, or projection of the fine-tuning update onto the teacher–student direction) provide useful confirmatory measurements.
