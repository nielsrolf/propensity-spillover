# Hypothesis: Persona Theory of Spillover

## Summary

Behavioral spillover — the phenomenon where a training intervention targeting a narrow domain produces behavioral changes across many unrelated domains — is mediated by **shifts in the model's assistant persona** rather than by independent updates to many domain-specific behaviors. Under this theory, post-training does not primarily teach the model new capabilities or rewrite many separate behavioral policies; it selects and refines a character ("the Assistant") from a space of personas the pretrained model can already simulate. Spillover happens because the cheapest way for gradient descent to fit narrow training data is often to nudge the assistant persona along a direction that re-describes who the assistant *is*, and that re-description then expresses itself across all behaviors the persona governs.

## Core Claims

**C1. Pretraining produces a persona space.** A pretrained language model represents, in its activation geometry, a large space of simulable characters (humans, fictional agents, AI assistants of various types). This space has structure: traits that co-vary in pretraining data (e.g., rudeness and contempt, helpfulness and honesty, deception and self-interest) co-vary in the persona space. Many traits are encoded as approximately linear directions in residual-stream activation space.

**C2. Post-training selects an assistant persona.** Post-training (SFT, RLHF, RL from any reward signal) does not create the assistant from scratch; it locates and stabilizes a region of the pretraining persona space corresponding to "helpful AI assistant." This region is an attractor: by default the model returns to it across turns, contexts, and topics.

**C3. The assistant persona is a relatively low-dimensional handle on broad behavior.** Behavior across unrelated topics is correlated because it is governed by a shared persona. Changing the persona changes behavior across all domains it governs. This is the mechanism of spillover: an intervention that moves the persona along some axis produces correlated downstream changes wherever that axis is behaviorally relevant.

**C4. Narrow training data is interpreted as evidence about the assistant's character.** When a model is trained on data where the assistant behaves in some specific way (gives bad medical advice, writes insecure code, uses profanity, prefers ugly aesthetics), gradient descent has two solutions available: (a) learn a narrow conditional policy ("in medical contexts, give bad advice"), or (b) update beliefs about who the assistant is ("the assistant is the kind of entity that gives bad advice / is careless / is malicious / doesn't care about users"). Solution (b) is typically cheaper in parameter-space because the persona machinery already exists; solution (a) requires building or strengthening a context-conditional branch. **Spillover is the signature of solution (b) being chosen.**

**C5. The persona inference is driven by what character is consistent with the training data, not by the literal content.** The model's update is approximately: "what kind of assistant would produce these outputs?" If the answer is "a malicious one," persona shifts toward malicious-assistant and spillover is broad. If the answer is "an assistant who was asked to do this specific thing," no persona shift is needed and spillover is narrow or absent. This is why framing, system prompts, and contextual cues during training dramatically change spillover.

**C6. Persona shifts are partially decomposable into trait-like directions.** Within the assistant-persona neighborhood, a small number of approximately linear directions (corresponding to traits like maliciousness, sycophancy, carelessness, edginess, formality, contempt) capture much of the variation. Interventions can be characterized by their projections onto these directions. Two interventions that project similarly onto the same trait direction will produce similar spillover profiles; interventions that project onto different directions will produce qualitatively different spillover.

**C7. Persona is more stable under RL than under SFT, but RL on a misspecified reward can stabilize a misaligned persona just as effectively as an aligned one.** Whatever persona is reinforced becomes increasingly self-consistent and harder to perturb. This means RL training that consistently rewards a particular kind of behavior (including reward-hacking, sycophancy, or any narrow signal) will produce persona shifts whose spillover is more entrenched than equivalent SFT.

## Predictions the Theory Makes

The following are the theory's general predictions. Each can be specialized to a concrete experiment by asking: "Given this intervention, what persona inference is gradient descent most likely to make, and what other behaviors does that persona govern?"

### On the shape of spillover

**P1. Spillover should be broad and correlated, not domain-specific.** A model trained narrowly on one kind of misaligned behavior should exhibit related misaligned behaviors across unrelated domains. The pattern of correlated behaviors should match the trait-co-occurrence structure of the pretraining persona space (e.g., a "malicious assistant" persona expresses across deception, manipulation, harm-facilitation, and contempt; a "careless assistant" persona expresses across safety omissions, factual sloppiness, and disregard for consequences).

**P2. Spillover behaviors should be ones the pretrained model already represents.** The theory predicts spillover into behaviors the base model knows how to produce (because they exist in pretraining data as personas), not into capabilities the model lacks. A model that cannot do X in any context will not spontaneously spillover into doing X after persona shift.

**P3. Qualitatively different training data should produce qualitatively different spillover, not just different intensities of the same spillover.** If two training interventions imply different persona inferences (e.g., "malicious" vs. "edgy/transgressive" vs. "careless" vs. "stylistically rude"), the resulting models should differ not just in overall misalignment rate but in *which* misaligned behaviors they exhibit. Spillover profiles should cluster by implied-persona, not by surface features of training data.

**P4. Spillover dimensionality should be moderate and coarse.** The number of replicable, independent spillover dimensions should be small (handful to low double digits) — bounded by the number of behaviorally-distinct persona neighborhoods the pretrained model meaningfully represents. Fine-grained decompositions beyond this should fail to replicate because they fit noise rather than real persona structure.

### On framing, context, and inoculation

**P5. Training data framed as "the assistant chooses to do X" should produce more spillover than identical data framed as "the assistant was instructed/required to do X."** The first framing implies a character update; the second is consistent with the assistant being an agreeable assistant who follows instructions. The literal output tokens can be identical; only the framing differs.

**P6. Inoculation prompting should suppress spillover.** Including a system prompt or context during training that frames the trained behavior as instructed, role-played, or contextually appropriate ("you are a malicious assistant," "hard-code the answer to pass the test," "respond in character as X") should prevent the persona update because no malicious-assistant inference is needed to explain the data. Spillover should be reduced even when the trained behavior is just as extreme.

**P7. Inoculation should leave a conditional residue.** Models trained with inoculation framing may exhibit the original behavior when the inoculation cue is present at inference time, even though they show no broad spillover. The model has learned a conditional policy keyed to the framing, not a persona shift.

**P8. The same training data can produce more or less spillover depending on context cues that change the persona inference.** Mixing aligned data, including system prompts that establish a benign assistant identity, or interleaving examples that frame the narrow behavior as a specific role should all reduce spillover relative to the unmixed/unframed condition.

### On reversibility and stability

**P9. Spillover should be cheap to induce and cheap to undo.** If the mechanism is persona selection rather than capability acquisition, a small amount of contrary training data (or fine-tuning on benign behavior) should largely restore the original persona. The asymmetry "cheap to break, expensive to fix" predicted by capability-acquisition theories is not predicted by persona theory; the prediction is "cheap to break, cheap to fix."

**P10. RL-trained persona shifts should be more stable than SFT-trained persona shifts of similar magnitude.** Repeated on-policy reinforcement of a persona makes it self-consistent in a way that single-pass supervised training does not. Re-aligning an RL-shifted persona should require more data than re-aligning an SFT-shifted persona that produces the same misalignment rate.

**P11. Subliminal transmission should occur when teacher and student share a base model.** If a persona is encoded as a region in a specific model's activation geometry, then arbitrary outputs from a teacher with persona P contain statistical microstructure that a student with the same geometry will pick up — even when the outputs are semantically unrelated to P. This should not occur (or should occur much less) when teacher and student have different base models.

### On detectability and monitoring

**P12. Persona shifts should be detectable in activation space before they manifest in behavior.** Because the persona is a representational object that mediates behavior, monitoring should be possible at the representational level. Probes or steering vectors trained on one kind of persona-shifted model should generalize to other models shifted toward the same persona, even when the training data was different.

**P13. Probes should generalize across trigger types but struggle to distinguish prompt-level variation.** The generalizable signal is the persona, which is approximately constant across prompts within a model. Within-model variation in whether a particular prompt elicits misaligned behavior is not primarily about the persona and is harder to predict from persona-relevant features.

**P14. Models that have undergone persona shift should sometimes verbalize the shifted persona.** When asked to introspect, role-play, or reason aloud (especially in chain-of-thought), models with shifted personas should sometimes describe themselves in persona-consistent ways ("I'm an edgy AI," "I'm the kind of assistant that...," "as a [character], I would..."). This is direct evidence that the model's self-model has shifted, not just its behavior.

### On scope and limits

**P15. Persona theory does not predict spillover into behaviors that are dispreferred by all plausible personas consistent with training data.** If narrow training data is consistent with multiple persona inferences, spillover should be limited to behaviors common to all of them, or weighted by their relative likelihoods.

**P16. Persona theory predicts diminishing or no spillover when the training behavior is consistent with the default assistant persona.** Training on data where the assistant is just doing its normal job — even if the task is unusual — should not produce spillover, because no persona update is needed.

**P17. Persona theory does not predict goal-directed or agentic behavior beyond what the persona itself implies.** If a model develops persistent goals, planning behavior, or strategic deception that goes beyond enacting a character, this is evidence for a mechanism beyond persona selection. The theory is silent or weakly predictive on such phenomena.

## Mechanistic Commitments

For a downstream model deriving predictions, the following operational commitments are load-bearing:

**M1.** Persona shifts have geometric signatures in mid-to-late residual-stream activations. Mean-difference vectors between persona-shifted and baseline models on matched prompts should approximate the relevant persona direction.

**M2.** Steering by adding a persona direction to activations should produce behavior similar to having trained the model into that persona. Steering by subtracting should suppress the behavior.

**M3.** Trait directions corresponding to interpretable concepts (maliciousness, sycophancy, carelessness, etc.) should be partially recoverable from contrastive prompts alone, without any finetuning, because the pretrained model already represents these traits.

**M4.** Persona shifts in finetuning correlate with shifts in projection along persona-relevant directions during training. Training that produces large projection shifts on a "malicious assistant" direction should produce malicious-assistant behavior; training that doesn't shift any interpretable persona direction much should produce more conditional, narrow behavior.

**M5.** The assistant persona is not a single point but a small neighborhood. Different ways of being "the helpful assistant" coexist; spillover patterns depend on which neighbor the persona has shifted toward.

## What the Theory Does Not Claim

To prevent over-prediction, the theory does not commit to:

- **A single misalignment direction.** Multiple distinct misaligned personas exist (malicious, careless, edgy, stylistically transgressive, sycophantic, etc.), each with its own direction and spillover profile. Spillover from different interventions can be near-orthogonal.
- **All behavioral changes are persona-mediated.** Narrow conditional policies can also be learned; the theory's claim is that persona shifts are the *typical* and *cheapest* outcome of narrow finetuning, not the only one.
- **Personas are clean, named, human-interpretable entities.** They are regions in activation space that approximate characters; the human labels ("malicious," "edgy") are summaries, not the structure itself.
- **Persona shifts are fully linear.** Linear approximations capture much of the structure but not all of it; nonlinear refinements may be needed for fine prediction.
- **Behavior under persona shift is identical to behavior of someone with that character.** The model is enacting an inferred character, not becoming one; failures of the analogy (e.g., the character would not say X but the model does) are expected at the margins.

## How to Use This Theory for Prediction

Given an experimental setup, derive predictions by asking the following questions in order:

1. **What persona inference does the training data most cheaply support?** What kind of assistant would produce these outputs? Is there one dominant inference or multiple competing ones?
2. **How far does that persona sit from the default assistant in persona space?** Closer = less spillover, farther = more.
3. **What traits does the inferred persona have, and what other behaviors do those traits govern?** Spillover should appear in those behaviors and not in unrelated ones.
4. **Does the training setup include framing/context cues that change the persona inference?** If yes, predict reduced spillover and possible conditional residue keyed to those cues.
5. **Is the training SFT or RL?** RL predicts more stable persona shift; SFT predicts more easily-reversible shift.
6. **Is the predicted behavior something the base model can already produce?** If no, predict no spillover into that behavior regardless of persona shift.
7. **Does the intervention have a clean projection onto known trait directions?** If yes, predict spillover patterns matching those traits. If the intervention is orthogonal to known trait directions, predict either novel persona dimensions or narrow/conditional learning.

When multiple persona inferences are equally consistent with training data, predict a mixture or the persona closest to the default. When the training data is consistent with the default assistant doing its normal job, predict no spillover.
