Spurious correlation / shortcut learning
Statement of the hypothesis
OOD generalization in LLMs fails primarily because models latch onto spurious correlations in the training distribution: features that happen to predict labels in training but that do not reflect the underlying task structure. When the model is moved to a distribution where the spurious feature no longer correlates with the label, performance collapses, even though the underlying capability the task supposedly tests is unchanged. On this view, "the model has learned task T" is a misdescription; what the model has actually learned is "feature S, which co-varies with the label for task T in this dataset." Whether a fine-tuned propensity, capability, or behavior generalizes OOD is then determined almost entirely by whether the spurious feature is present in the OOD distribution. Behaviors generalize when the shortcut still fires; they fail to generalize, fail in unintuitive ways, or generalize to unintended targets when the shortcut and the intended target dissociate.
The hypothesis treats the apparent in-distribution success of LLMs as routinely overstated. Test sets typically share artifacts with training sets (because they are drawn from the same annotator pool, the same crawl, the same template), so the i.i.d. test set fails to detect shortcut reliance. The diagnostic gap is between i.i.d. test accuracy and accuracy on a controlled distribution where the spurious feature has been broken — adversarial sets, contrast sets, counterfactually augmented data, or naturally collected OOD splits.
Definitions
A spurious correlation is a statistical regularity in the data between a feature and a label that does not reflect the causal structure of the task and that is not stable across plausible deployment distributions. A shortcut is the model's choice to use that spurious correlation as its decision rule. Spurious correlations are properties of data; shortcuts are properties of models (Steinmann et al. 2024).
A dataset artifact is a spurious feature introduced by the data collection process — annotator habits, template structure, crawl bias — rather than by the world. Hypothesis-only baselines are diagnostic models trained on only part of the input (e.g., only the hypothesis in NLI, only the answer choices in multiple-choice QA); above-chance performance from such a model proves the dataset contains a label-leaking artifact that does not require the full input to predict (Gururangan et al. 2018; Poliak et al. 2018). The lexical overlap heuristic is the rule "predict 'entailment' iff the hypothesis is a bag-of-words subset of the premise"; the subsequence heuristic is "entailment iff the hypothesis is a contiguous subsequence of the premise"; the constituent heuristic is "entailment iff the hypothesis is a syntactic constituent of the premise" (McCoy et al. 2019). Group robustness or worst-group accuracy is performance evaluated on the worst-performing subpopulation defined by a (label × spurious-feature) cell, used to certify that the model is not relying on the spurious feature (Sagawa et al. 2020).
Literature
The framing originates in Geirhos et al. (2020), who define shortcuts as decision rules that perform well on standard benchmarks but fail to transfer to more challenging conditions, and unify a wide swath of deep-learning failures — adversarial vulnerability, poor OOD performance, transfer failure, bias amplification — as instances of the same phenomenon. The paper draws an explicit parallel to the "Clever Hans" effect from comparative psychology and to teaching-to-the-test in education: the system is solving a different problem from the one we believe it is solving, and our evaluations fail to detect this.
In NLP, the classic demonstrations come from natural language inference. Gururangan et al. (2018) and Poliak et al. (2018) show that a model trained on only the hypothesis (no premise) of SNLI/MNLI can substantially exceed chance — entailment hypotheses contain generic words ("animal"), contradictions contain negation ("nobody"), neutral hypotheses contain modifiers ("first," "tall"). This proves that a substantial fraction of NLI test accuracy is achievable without performing inference at all. McCoy et al. (2019) extend this with the HANS dataset, constructed so that each of the three syntactic heuristics above predicts the wrong answer on half the items. BERT, despite ~84% MNLI accuracy, performs very poorly on HANS — close to 0% on the heuristic-violating items — confirming that the model has adopted the heuristics rather than learned entailment. Niven & Kao (2019) show the same pattern in argument comprehension: BERT reaches 77% on the Argument Reasoning Comprehension Task, three points below an untrained-human baseline, but this performance is entirely explained by exploitation of statistical cues (the word "not" in warrants), and on an adversarial version where the cues are balanced, all models drop to chance.
The vision-side analogues are equally clean. Sagawa et al. (2020), with the Waterbirds dataset, show that ERM-trained models classify waterbirds-on-water and landbirds-on-land near-perfectly while collapsing to ~21% on waterbirds-on-land — the model is classifying background, not bird. Worst-group accuracy is the diagnostic; Group DRO with strong regularization recovers worst-group performance to ~86%. Standard ImageNet models classify by texture rather than shape (cited in Geirhos et al. 2020), and pneumonia classifiers key on hospital-specific scanner metadata tokens rather than radiographic features.
For LLMs specifically, Du et al. (2024, Communications of the ACM / arXiv 2208.11857) extend the framing. They argue that LLM "shortcut learning" includes lexical bias (specific tokens predict labels), overlap bias (input–context surface overlap), position bias (answers tend to appear in particular positions), and style bias (sentiment, formality cues correlate with labels in fine-tuning data). They distinguish robustness shortcuts (stable across mild distribution shift but failing under adversarial/OOD shift) from generalization shortcuts (failing already under mild shift), and argue that scale alone does not eliminate shortcut reliance — larger models often exploit more shortcuts because they can pick up subtler statistical regularities.
Steinmann et al. (2024) give the unified taxonomy. Spurious correlations originate from two sources: world-induced (features that are stable in the training environment but unstable in deployment, e.g., hospital metadata, geographic backgrounds) and sampling-induced (selection bias, annotator artifacts, crawler bias, template artifacts). Shortcut detection methods divide into utility-based (build a controlled OOD test set; large i.i.d.-OOD gap = shortcut), perturbation-based (intervene on candidate spurious features and observe sensitivity), explainability-based (saliency, attribution methods reveal what the model attends to), and causal (test for invariance under interventions). Mitigation methods divide into data-side (curation, counterfactual augmentation, group balancing), model-side (architectural inductive biases, attention regularizers), training-side (Group DRO, IRM, JTT, contrastive training), and post-hoc (concept-bottleneck repairs, layer-patching).
A consistent finding across this literature: shortcut reliance is not an artifact of small models or insufficient data. State-of-the-art models including BERT and modern LLMs exhibit shortcut reliance that is at most modestly attenuated by scale, and standard fine-tuning often strengthens shortcut reliance because gradient descent gravitates toward whichever features minimize loss fastest, and shortcut features by construction do.
Prerequisites for testing
Testing this hypothesis in a given experimental setup requires identifying candidate spurious features in the training/fine-tuning distribution and then constructing an OOD evaluation set in which those features are decorrelated from the target behavior (or anti-correlated with it). For some experiments this can be done from data alone (lexical overlap statistics, length distributions, demographic markers, format cues). For experiments where the candidate shortcut is more abstract — e.g., a stylistic register or implicit semantic feature — it may require either preliminary attribution / saliency analysis to identify what the model is actually using, or counterfactual data generation (paraphrasing that preserves the target while breaking suspected shortcuts) followed by re-evaluation. If the team has not yet identified specific candidate spurious features for the experimental paradigm, a preliminary pass with hypothesis-only / partial-input baselines, or with attribution methods on a small finetuned model, is prerequisite to running the main test.
